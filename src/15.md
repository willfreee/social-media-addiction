# Watching Other People Live

Think about the last time you watched a video of someone traveling. They were walking through a foreign city, or eating something incredible at a street market, or standing on a beach at sunset with the waves rolling in and the sky turning gold. The video was beautiful. It gave you a feeling — a little lift, a little ache, a little sense of being somewhere else for a moment.

Now think about where you were. Sitting on a couch. Lying in bed. Standing in line. Motionless except for one thumb. The room around you was dark, or quiet, or filled with the ordinary clutter of your actual life. And while the person on the screen walked through a city on the other side of the world, you didn't move. You didn't go anywhere. You just watched.

That video gave you something — a sensation, an emotion, a brief escape from wherever you were. But did it give you an experience? Did your lungs fill with sea air? Did your feet touch foreign ground? Did your actual life, the one happening in the room around you, change in any way? Or did you just borrow the feeling of someone else's experience while your own life stood perfectly, completely still?

This is the last of the Big Monster's illusions. It's quieter than the others, harder to name, easier to dismiss as harmless. It's the belief that watching other people live is a form of living. That consuming their adventures, their relationships, their creative work, their daily routines — that this expands your own life, gives you access to worlds you wouldn't otherwise see. That it's not the same as doing those things yourself, maybe, but it's a meaningful substitute when you can't.

It's not. It's the opposite. And this chapter is going to show you why.

### The Body That Didn't Move

Let's start with the physical reality of what happens when you scroll. Not the emotional experience, not the content, not the story you tell yourself about what you're doing. Just the physical facts.

You are sitting or lying down. Your body is motionless except for one thumb. Your eyes are fixed on a small rectangle of light. The room around you — the actual world you inhabit — is unchanged. The air is the same temperature it was an hour ago. The light outside your window has shifted slightly with the passage of time you didn't notice. Your body hasn't moved. Your hands haven't made anything. Your voice hasn't spoken to anyone. Nothing has happened in your physical reality except the passage of time and the gradual depletion of your attention.

Meanwhile, on the screen: people are traveling. Dancing. Cooking elaborate meals. Laughing with friends. Embracing partners. Creating art. Building businesses. Walking through forests and swimming in oceans and living, vividly, in full motion. The contrast is so stark it's almost absurd, and yet we've trained ourselves not to notice it. The feed is full of life. The viewer is still. The feed is full of experience. The viewer is consuming footage of experience while having none.

If you spent an hour watching a movie about people eating a magnificent feast — course after course, the camera lingering on every dish, the actors expressing delight with every bite — would you say you'd "had dinner"? Your mouth didn't taste anything. Your stomach didn't fill. You just watched other people eat. You could describe the meal in detail. You could recall the look of the food, the sounds of enjoyment, the atmosphere of the dining room. But you didn't eat. You're still hungry. The watching gave you the sensation of dining without any of the substance. And calling that sensation "dinner" would be a strange misuse of the word.

Why do we call watching other people live "living vicariously" instead of "not living for an hour while staring at footage of people who are"? The "vicarious" makes it sound like a variation of living, a different flavor of experience, a legitimate alternative when the real thing isn't available. But there's nothing alternative about it. It's the absence of living. It's stillness. The person on the screen moved through the world. You stayed exactly where you were. Their life expanded. Yours paused. The hour passed, and at the end of it, they had an experience and you had a memory of watching them have one.

### The Loan You've Been Taking Out

The feed is full of people whose lives look better than yours. Richer, more exciting, more beautiful, more achieved. Travelers waking up in places you've only seen in photographs. Creators whose work is admired by thousands. Couples whose love looks effortless and golden. Fitness influencers whose bodies seem to have been designed by someone with a clearer vision than whatever forces shaped your own. Lifestyle bloggers whose homes are always clean, whose mornings are always serene, whose coffee is always artfully lit.

You follow them. You watch their content. And it gives you something — a genuine emotional experience. Aspiration. Envy. The sensation of being close to something desirable. For a moment, watching their lives, you feel like you're participating in a world more vivid than your own. You're borrowing the feeling of a life well-lived.

But a loan has terms. And the terms of the Borrowed Life are brutal.

Every hour you spend watching someone else's life is an hour you didn't spend living your own. The traveler explored a new city while you watched from your couch. The creator made something while you consumed what they made. The couple built a relationship while you observed its curated highlights. The fitness influencer moved their body while yours stayed still. You borrowed the sensation of their experience, and you paid for it with your actual time — the hours, the attention, the energy you could have spent creating experiences of your own.

If you borrowed money every month to feel wealthy, and the interest kept growing, at what point would the borrowed feeling of wealth be outweighed by the debt? The first month, it might feel like a revelation — you have access to a lifestyle you couldn't otherwise afford. The second month, the payments start. The third month, you're working to service the debt, not to build anything of your own. By the end of the year, you've borrowed so much that the gap between the life you're pretending to have and the life you actually have has become a chasm.

The Borrowed Life works exactly the same way. You borrow the sensation of experience — the travel, the creativity, the romance, the adventure — and you pay for it with your actual life. The debt accumulates in hours. In years. In the growing distance between the person you watch and the person you are. The feed gives you the feeling of a rich, varied, exciting existence, and it takes as payment the only thing you have that's actually valuable: your time. Your attention. Your one and only life, spent watching someone else's.

### The Stranger Who Doesn't Know Your Name

And it's not just strangers. Or rather, they start as strangers, but they don't stay that way — not in your mind. You follow someone for months, for years. You learn their partner's name. Their pet's name. The layout of their apartment. Their morning routine. Their career struggles. Their mental health journey. Their taste in music, in food, in furniture. You've watched them cry. You've watched them celebrate. You've watched them grow and change and evolve. You feel like you know them. The intimacy feels real.

But do they know you exist? If you passed them on the street, would they recognize you? If you commented on their post and they liked it — that tiny heart, that micro-moment of acknowledgment — did that feel like connection? Did it feel like being seen?

This is the parasocial relationship, and it's one of the strangest features of the Borrowed Life. The creator broadcasts. You consume. Over time, the consumption creates a sense of intimacy — the human brain is wired to respond to faces, voices, emotional expression, personal disclosure. These are the building blocks of real connection, and when they're delivered through a screen, the brain processes them in much the same way it processes actual social interaction. The warmth you feel toward the person on the screen is genuine. The sense that you know them is genuine. But it's entirely one-directional.

If you sat in a theater every night and watched the same actor perform a play about their life — and over time, you learned everything about their character, their stories, their emotions — would you say you had a relationship with that actor? You'd know their mannerisms. You'd recognize their voice anywhere. You'd feel genuine care for their character's struggles and genuine joy at their character's triumphs. But the actor doesn't know your name. The actor has never seen your face. The intimacy is real on your side and nonexistent on theirs. That's not a relationship. That's an audience.

The parasocial relationships you've built through the feed are not connections. They're performances you've mistaken for connection. The hunger underneath — the real, valid hunger to know and be known, to matter to someone, to share the intimate details of life with another person — has been fed a simulation. A broadcast. A one-way transmission that feels like intimacy but lacks the single element that makes intimacy real: reciprocity. The person on the screen doesn't know you. And the you that has been pouring attention and emotional energy into knowing them has been giving that energy to a mirage.

### The Performance That Called Itself Real

But surely, you might think, what I'm seeing is real. These are real people. Sharing real moments from their real lives. It may be one-directional, but it's authentic. It's not a performance — it's just someone living their life and letting me watch.

Consider what you're actually seeing when you watch an influencer's content. The beautiful, sunlit apartment — filmed after an hour of cleaning and staging, with the dirty dishes and the piles of mail and the clutter of actual living pushed just outside the frame. The spontaneous, joyful moment — captured on the seventh take, after the lighting was adjusted and the angle was found and the spontaneous line was delivered three times before it felt spontaneous enough. The "candid" conversation between partners — discussed beforehand, framed for the camera, edited afterward. The vulnerability, the authenticity, the "real talk" about struggles and mental health — genuine in content, perhaps, but selected, timed, captioned, and packaged for maximum engagement.

This isn't to say the people you follow are lying. Most of them aren't. They're doing what anyone does when they know they're being watched: they're performing. The performance may be authentic in its way — they really are sharing real things — but it's still a performance. It's life transformed into content. And content is not life. It's a representation of life, optimized for the medium, filtered through the creator's self-presentation goals and the platform's algorithmic demands.

If you watched a reality TV show that was filmed in someone's actual house, with their actual family, and edited to look like their actual life — would you say you "knew what their life was like"? You'd have seen real people in a real house doing real things. But you'd also know, on some level, that the presence of cameras changed what was happening. That the editing shaped the story. That what you were watching was a show about a family, not the family itself. The difference between a life and a show about a life is everything. And every account you follow is a show about a life. The set is real. The actors are playing themselves. But it's still a show.

You haven't been seeing how other people live. You've been seeing how other people perform living for an audience. And mistaking the performance for reality — feeling inadequate next to it, measuring your own behind-the-scenes against someone else's highlight reel — is a game you were never going to win, because the game was rigged from the start.

### The Piano You Never Played

But it's just entertainment, the Big Monster says. It's not like I'd be doing something else with that time. I'm relaxing. I'm unwinding. It's harmless.

Let's do the math. Not in a guilt-inducing way — not as a stick to beat yourself with — but as a genuine inquiry. A clear-eyed look at what the Borrowed Life has cost.

Think about the hours. The years of scrolling. The thousands of hours spent watching other people travel, create, connect, exercise, cook, decorate, dance, and live. Now ask yourself: what could have happened in those hours if they hadn't been spent watching? Not in the sense of "you wasted your life" — that's the old voice, the shame voice, the one that makes you feel bad and then drives you right back to the feed to escape the bad feeling. But simply, neutrally, what could those hours have held?

What skills could have been developed — a language, an instrument, a craft, a sport? What creative work could have been produced — writing, drawing, building, designing, the thing you've been meaning to start for years? What real connections could have been deepened — conversations with people you love, time spent in the same room without screens between you, the slow, unglamorous work of actually being present in your relationships? What experiences — actual, firsthand, un-curated experiences — could have been lived? What places could you have gone? What could your body have done, felt, seen, touched, tasted?

The feed didn't just fill time. It replaced time. Every hour spent watching was an hour not spent living. And the cumulative weight of that replacement is not just a collection of missed opportunities — it's a version of you that didn't get to exist. The pianist you didn't become. The traveler you didn't become. The creator, the friend, the partner, the person you didn't become — not because you lacked the capacity, but because the time that could have shaped you into them was spent watching other people be who they became.

If you spent three hours a day for ten years watching videos of people playing piano — watching their fingers, feeling inspired by their music, knowing their repertoires by heart — at the end of the decade, would you know how to play piano? Or would you just have watched ten thousand hours of someone else playing, while your own hands never touched the keys? The knowledge would be there. The inspiration would be there. But the skill — the thing that only comes from doing — would be absent. Because watching is not doing. Consuming is not creating. Borrowing is not living.

### The Inspiration That Never Became Action

But wait, you might say. It's not just passive. It's aspirational. Watching people who are living the life I want is motivating. It shows me what's possible. It inspires me to work toward my own goals. That's not nothing. That has value.

And sometimes, it's true. A video does spark something. A post does shift your perspective. A creator's journey does make you believe, for a moment, that you could do it too. That spark is real. The feeling of possibility is real.

But what happens next? What happens after the spark?

You feel the inspiration. You save the post. You tell yourself *I'm going to do this. I'm going to start.* And then you keep scrolling. The next video appears. The next post. The next spark. And the inspiration from the first one is already fading, replaced by a new inspiration, a new possibility, a new thing you're going to start someday. The inspiration was real for as long as it lasted. But it didn't last. It couldn't — because the feed is designed to replace every piece of content with another piece of content, every feeling with another feeling, every spark with another spark. The inspiration was just another kind of content. Consumed, registered, and scrolled past.

Inspiration without action is just entertainment with a nicer name. If a video makes you feel like you could do something, and then you close the app and do nothing, was it inspiration — or was it the sensation of inspiration, consumed and discarded like everything else in the feed? The spark was real, but a spark that doesn't catch fire is just a brief light in the dark. And the feed is very good at providing sparks — and even better at ensuring they never catch.

The life you want — the one the influencers are living, or appearing to live — is not in the feed. It's on the other side of the action you've been replacing with watching. The traveler didn't become a traveler by watching travel videos. The creator didn't become a creator by consuming other people's creations. The fit person didn't get fit by watching workout content. They did the thing. And the doing — the unglamorous, un-postable, behind-the-scenes doing — is what the feed never shows you and what the Borrowed Life can never provide.

### Your Life, Unborrowed

This is the part where it would be easy to feel regret. To look back at the years of borrowed living and feel the weight of everything that didn't happen. But that's not what this chapter is for. Regret is just another form of passivity — another way of staring at the past instead of moving in the present. And the Borrowed Life's final cost would be to make you spend even more time, even more attention, mourning the life you didn't live instead of starting to live it now.

So let's not do that. Let's look forward instead.

Your life — the one you've been escaping into the feed to avoid — is not boring compared to the content you consume. It's uncurated. It's unedited. It's real. The quiet moments, the small joys, the messy imperfections, the genuine connections that happen off-camera, the slow accumulation of skill and experience and relationship that comes from doing rather than watching — these are the substance of a life actually lived. The feed made them feel inadequate by comparison to performances. But performances are not lives. And your life, however ordinary it may seem next to an influencer's highlight reel, has something no Borrowed Life can ever provide: it's yours. It's happening. And it's waiting for you to show up.

If you compared your behind-the-scenes footage to everyone else's highlight reel, you'd always feel like you were losing. But the highlight reel isn't real. Your behind-the-scenes is. The messy kitchen, the awkward conversation, the project that didn't turn out, the walk around the block that wasn't Instagram-worthy but still moved your body through the world — these are the materials of a life. They're not content. They're not performances. They're just yours. And every hour you spend watching someone else's highlight reel is an hour of your own behind-the-scenes you never get back.

The question isn't whether their life is more interesting than yours. The question is whether you're going to live yours or keep watching theirs.

Watching other people live is not a form of living. It's a form of stillness — consuming footage of motion while your own life passes unexperienced. The feed didn't expand your world. It borrowed you the sensation of experience while you paid with your actual hours, your actual attention, your actual unlived life. The influencers, the creators, the strangers whose routines you know better than your own — they were never the point. The hunger was for your own experience. Your own creation. Your own life, unborrowed. And every hour you spent watching was an hour you didn't spend living it.

The feed doesn't give you access to life. It gives you a substitute that keeps you from the real thing. The real thing is still there. It's been there the whole time. And now, for the first time in a long time, you're about to have the space to actually live.
