8: But What About My Job?

So far, we've been dismantling illusions. Connection. Information. Relaxation. Inspiration. Each chapter has taken one of the Big Monster's core arguments and shown it for what it is — a story that doesn't survive scrutiny.

But you might have been reading all of this with a quiet objection running in the background. Something that feels different from the other arguments. Something that isn't an illusion at all.

*That's all well and good, but I actually need social media for my job. This isn't about emotional nourishment or false beliefs. This is a real requirement. I manage accounts. I post updates. I network with colleagues. I monitor industry news. I maintain a professional presence. If I walk away from these platforms, I'm not just giving up scrolling — I'm putting my career at risk.*

And you might be right. Let's talk about that.

---

### The Bartender and the Shot Glass

Let's start by acknowledging something the Big Monster doesn't usually get: your work use might be genuine. This book has been careful from the beginning to distinguish between conscious, chosen, utility-driven tool use and the compulsive orbit that forms around it. If you open an app to post a work update, or check a client DM, or research a contact, and you do those things and then close the app — that's not addiction. That's not the trap. That's using a tool.

But here's the question that separates the tool from the trap: when you open the app for work, what happens after the work is done?

You open LinkedIn to post a company update. The post takes two minutes. Maybe three. You've said what needed to be said. The task is complete. And then — what? Do you close the app? Or does your thumb drift? The feed is right there. Someone liked your post. Someone commented on a colleague's update. A notification badge is pulsing. An interesting headline catches your eye. Before you've even registered the shift, you're scrolling. Two minutes of work have become ten minutes of feed consumption. Twenty minutes. Twenty-five. You're looking at content that has nothing to do with your job, posted by people you've never met, and you're not entirely sure how you got there.

Imagine you work in a bar. Your job requires you to be around alcohol all day. You pour drinks. You serve customers. You manage inventory. The alcohol is genuinely part of your work — you can't do your job without being in its presence. But does "being around alcohol for work" explain why you're doing shots behind the bar at 2 p.m. on a Tuesday? Is the shot glass part of the job description? Or is there a difference between the work task and the drinking — and has the work task been giving the drinking a place to hide?

The legitimate work task is narrow, time-bound, and conscious. The compulsive orbit — checking notifications, scrolling the feed, drifting into content consumption — is not work. It's the Parasite using your job as an entry point. The fact that you sometimes need to be on social media for work doesn't explain or justify the 25 minutes of feed-scrolling that follow the 2-minute work task. Those 25 minutes are not your job. They're not networking. They're not professional development. They're the compulsion, wearing your job's ID badge, and the fact that the badge looks convincing doesn't mean you're on the clock.

---

### The Networking Superstition

But maybe you're thinking: *It's not just about posting updates and checking DMs. Networking happens in the feed. Commenting on colleagues' posts. Engaging with industry conversations. Staying visible. If I'm not scrolling, I'm not networking. I'll become invisible in my field.*

This is the Big Monster's most sophisticated professional argument. It takes a legitimate activity — building and maintaining professional relationships — and inflates it into a justification for constant feed presence. Let's look at what "networking in the feed" actually looks like.

You scroll through LinkedIn or X. You see a colleague's promotion announcement. You tap like. You scroll past an industry leader's hot take. You leave a thoughtful comment — something that shows you're engaged, informed, part of the conversation. You see a former coworker's post about a new job. You type "congrats!" and keep scrolling. You spend an hour doing this — liking, commenting, reacting, maintaining the ambient hum of professional visibility. It feels productive. It feels like relationship-building. It feels like the kind of thing successful people do.

Now, if you tracked every single one of those networking actions for a week — every like, every comment, every share — and then asked which ones led to a tangible professional outcome, what would that ratio look like? Not which ones felt good. Not which ones generated a reciprocal like or a brief reply. Which ones actually led to something — a new client, a job lead, a meaningful connection, an opportunity that wouldn't have existed otherwise?

For most people, the honest answer is almost none. The vast majority of "networking" activity in the feed produces nothing but a mild, fading sense of having been present. It's small talk at a conference that doesn't lead to a business card exchange. It's a handshake that's forgotten by the time you reach the next person in the room. It's presence mistaken for productivity — the superstition that being seen is the same thing as building something.

If you attended a networking event and spent two hours making small talk with everyone in the room — most of it forgotten by morning, none of it leading to anything concrete — and then pointed to those two hours as "essential business development," would you be networking? Or would you just be socializing in a room full of people with business cards, mistaking the sensation of busy interaction for genuine relationship-building? The event was real. The conversations were real. But if nothing came of them, was it strategy — or was it just a way to feel like you were working while avoiding the harder, more focused work of actually building something?

Genuine networking is intentional. You reach out to a specific person for a specific reason. You engage with a specific conversation because it matters to your work, not because it happened to appear in your feed. You build relationships through direct contact — emails, calls, meetings, introductions — not through ambient like-button maintenance. The tiny handful of feed interactions that actually lead to something could be done deliberately, in minutes, without the hour of scrolling that surrounds them. The networking that matters doesn't require the compulsion. The compulsion just uses "networking" as a cover story to keep the feed open.

---

### The Lottery Ticket

There's a deeper fear underneath the networking argument, and it's one the Big Monster returns to whenever the practical defenses start to crumble.

*I've heard the stories. People who got job offers through a DM. Freelancers who built six-figure businesses through Instagram. Professionals who landed dream roles through a LinkedIn connection. Someone whose post went viral and changed their entire career trajectory. These things happen. If I'm not constantly present, I'll miss my chance. The opportunity cost is too high.*

The stories are real. Nobody's disputing that. Opportunities do sometimes arrive through social media. A DM leads to a client. A post catches the right person's attention. A connection made in a comment thread turns into something tangible. These things happen, and when they do, they're held up as proof that constant presence is not just justified — it's essential.

But let's do the math the Big Monster doesn't want you to do. For every person who got a job offer through social media, how many thousands of people spent thousands of hours scrolling and got nothing? For every freelancer who built a business through Instagram, how many built businesses through referrals, word of mouth, cold outreach, repeat clients, or simply being excellent at their work — without ever scrolling a feed? For every career-making viral post, how many millions of posts were made, liked, shared, and forgotten, consuming the time and attention of their creators without producing any tangible result whatsoever?

Lottery winners are real. People do win the lottery. You've seen their photos — the giant checks, the smiling families, the life-changing sums. Their stories are genuine. But if someone told you they were spending three hours a day buying lottery tickets because "people win — it happens," would you call that a career strategy? Or would you call it a statistical misunderstanding dressed up as hope? The fact that opportunities sometimes come through social media doesn't mean constant scrolling is the best — or even a particularly effective — way to find them.

The vast majority of professional opportunities come through other channels. Referrals from people who know your work. Reputation built through demonstrated competence. Direct outreach — emails, calls, applications, conversations. Real-world relationships formed through shared projects, mutual contacts, or genuine connection. The feed is not the only pipeline to opportunity, and for most people, it's not even the most effective one. It's just the most visible one, because the success stories are amplified while the millions of hours of fruitless scrolling are invisible.

The fear of missing a lottery-ticket DM is not a reason to spend three hours a day at the slot machine. It's the Parasite's last line of defense — the one argument that can't be definitively disproven because it's always theoretically possible that the next scroll will be the one that changes everything. But "theoretically possible" is not the same thing as "likely" or "worth the cost." And the cost — all those hours, all that attention, all that passive consumption dressed up as professional vigilance — is enormous. The time you spend chasing rare opportunities in the feed is time not spent building real ones elsewhere. Every hour of scrolling for a lottery ticket is an hour not spent doing the work that would actually make you someone worth discovering.

---

### Clocking Out

At this point, you might be feeling something uncomfortable. Not resistance to the argument — the argument is fairly clear. But a sense of practical impossibility.

*Okay, maybe the work stuff and the compulsive stuff are different. But they're so tangled together. I've tried to separate them before. I open the app to post something or check a message, and before I know it I'm scrolling. The pull is too strong. The only way to address the compulsion would be to quit entirely — and I can't quit entirely because of my job. I'm stuck.*

This is the all-or-nothing bind the Big Monster loves. Either you keep using social media — feeds, scrolling, compulsion and all — or you quit entirely and sacrifice your career. There's no middle ground. The compulsion is the price of the utility. Pay it or lose everything.

But that bind is a false choice. You already know this, because you already do it with other tools. You open your email, respond to what needs responding to, and close it. You don't spend 25 minutes reading random newsletters and old threads every time you check your inbox. Or if you do, you recognize that as procrastination — a choice to avoid work, not an essential part of work communication. You don't confuse "checking email" with "getting lost in email." The tool and the compulsion around the tool are separate things, and you know they're separate.

What makes social media different? Not the tool — the ability to post, message, and research is genuinely useful. What's different is the compulsion around the tool, and the fact that the compulsion has been calling itself "work" for so long that you've stopped questioning it. But the work task and the compulsive orbit are separable. The fact that they've always arrived together doesn't mean they're the same thing. The post is work. The DM is work. The research is work. The 25 minutes of feed-scrolling that follow are not work. They're the Parasite. They're the Little Monster. They're the Big Monster telling stories about connection and relaxation and inspiration while you scroll past content that has nothing to do with your job.

You can keep the tool while starving the compulsion. One does not require the other. And the way you separate them isn't through willpower — that's the old approach, the one that's failed you every time. The way you separate them is through clarity. When the Big Monster's stories are fully dismantled — all of them, not just the ones we've covered so far — the "pull" of the feed changes. It weakens. It stops feeling like a gravitational force and starts feeling like what it is: a flicker. A twitch. A stray cat that stops coming when you stop feeding it.

---

### The Haunted House

If you're skeptical that clarity alone can separate work from compulsion, that's fair. You've tried to be disciplined before. You've opened the app "just for work" and ended up in the feed anyway. The pull felt overwhelming. You're not convinced that seeing through a few illusions is going to change that.

And you're right — right now, it probably won't. Because the Big Monster's stories aren't fully dismantled yet. The Little Monster is still trained. The beliefs are still partially intact. When you open the app for work, a part of you still half-believes the feed contains connection, relaxation, information, inspiration — things you don't want to lose. That part of you keeps the door open. And the moment the work task is done, the compulsion walks through it.

But what we're doing in this book isn't about building stronger doors. It's about removing the belief that there's anything worth walking through for. When the Big Monster's stories have fully collapsed — when you see clearly that the feed contains no genuine connection, no real relaxation, no meaningful information, no actionable inspiration — the "pull" dissolves. Not because you got better at resisting it. Because the belief that powered it is gone.

If you'd spent years believing a haunted house was full of ghosts, and someone walked you through it room by room, showing you that every creak was old pipes, every shadow was a tree branch against the window, every cold draft was a gap in the insulation — would you still feel afraid when you walked through the front door? Or would the fear have dissolved, not because you got braver, but because the belief that powered it was gone? You don't need courage to enter a house you know is empty. You just need to have seen the evidence.

The Big Monster's stories are the ghosts. The feed is the house. And every chapter of this book is another room, another light switched on, another creak explained. By the time we're done, the house will be empty. You'll open the app, do the work task, and closing it will feel natural — not a battle you're winning, not a temptation you're resisting, just the obvious thing to do when the task is complete. Like clocking out at the end of a shift. You don't linger at your desk after work, staring at the wall, just because the building is still there. You leave. Because the work is done, and the building has nothing else for you.

I may genuinely need social media for specific, time-bound work tasks — posting, messaging, researching. The book has no argument with that. But the work task is not the compulsion, and the compulsion is not required for my career. The 25 minutes of feed-scrolling that follow the 2-minute work task are not my job. They're the Parasite using my job as a cover story. I can keep the tool — the narrow, conscious, intentional use — while starving the compulsion that has been riding on its back. One does not require the other. And once the Big Monster's stories are fully dismantled, closing the app when the work is done won't feel like a sacrifice. It will feel like clocking out.
